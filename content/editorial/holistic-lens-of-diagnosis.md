# Holistic Lens of Diagnosis: Understanding the person beyond the diagnostic label

**Clinical Commentary · Kizito Mbaekwe, MSN, APRN, PMHNP-BC**

In a busy clinical setting, diagnosis can become the finish line of an encounter. We listen to the symptoms, consider possible causes, document a working diagnosis, and initiate treatment. Then we move to the next patient.

But I think we need to ask ourselves a harder question: Have we understood the person, or have we simply found a diagnosis that fits the symptoms?

## George Engel’s biopsychosocial model

A diagnostic label serves an important purpose. It gives patients and clinicians a shared language and can guide treatment, referrals, and documentation. Yet it cannot, by itself, explain the forces shaping a person’s health. George Engel’s biopsychosocial model drew attention to the interaction of biological, psychological, and social influences on illness (Engel, 1977). Nearly five decades later, that perspective remains relevant to everyday clinical decisions.

## When symptoms tell only part of the story

Consider a hypothetical adult presenting with low mood, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, and disrupted sleep. A depressive disorder may be an appropriate working diagnosis, and treatment supported by clinical evidence may be warranted. Yet the assessment is incomplete if we never consider whether obstructive sleep apnea, thyroid disease, chronic pain, medication effects, or substance use might also contribute. We must also explore psychosocial stressors, past episodes suggestive of mania or hypomania, and the patient’s daily functioning when clinically indicated.

Several explanations may be true at the same time. A patient can have depression and a sleep disorder. Financial insecurity can intensify symptoms without being the sole cause of them. Conversely, some medical conditions or medications can produce symptoms that resemble psychiatric illness.

This does not mean every patient needs an extensive laboratory workup. Good clinical reasoning involves choosing tests and referrals based on the history, examination, risks, and likely value of the findings. It also means treating important symptoms promptly while keeping an appropriate plan for reassessment. The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2015) describes diagnosis as a process that develops over time through information gathering, interpretation, and collaboration.

## What makes comprehensive diagnosis difficult?

## Time, productivity, and reimbursement

A new evaluation may require a review of previous treatment, medication and substance exposure, sleep, medical conditions, trauma history, family circumstances, and functional impairment. We also have to assess immediate risks, explain our reasoning, and agree on a plan with the patient.

Short appointments, productivity targets, coverage restrictions, and administrative requirements can compete with that work. The effects vary across clinical settings, but the tension is real: the time needed for thoughtful assessment is not always reflected in how encounters are scheduled or reimbursed. The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2015) identified payment systems, liability, technology, and organizational conditions as influences on diagnostic quality.

One practical response is to match appointment length to clinical complexity. Complex initial assessments and diagnostic reviews may require protected time. Previsit questionnaires can gather useful information in advance, leaving more of the encounter for clarification and clinical judgment.

## Electronic records and fragmented information

Electronic health records can improve access to clinical information, but the presence of detailed templates does not guarantee a complete assessment. A clinician can complete every field in an electronic note and still miss an important contributor to the presenting symptoms.

Medication reconciliation, review of relevant external records, communication with referring clinicians, and clear responsibility for follow up are often more valuable than adding another checklist. When records remain scattered across organizations, important findings may not reach the person making the next treatment decision. Interoperability is useful, but information exchange must be followed by clinical communication and action.

Documentation technology, including speech recognition and artificial intelligence assisted tools, may support workflow. Whatever tool is used, the clinician remains responsible for reviewing the record, correcting errors, protecting confidentiality, and ensuring that the assessment reflects what actually occurred.

## Anchoring and diagnostic overshadowing

Clinical judgment is vulnerable to cognitive bias. Anchoring occurs when an initial impression is given too much weight. A related concern is diagnostic overshadowing, in which a known condition becomes the default explanation for new symptoms. For example, a history of anxiety should not automatically explain every episode of palpitations or breathlessness.

Croskerry (2003) examined cognitive errors in diagnosis and discussed reflective strategies for recognizing them. In practice, we can ask what findings do not fit the leading explanation, whether a potentially serious alternative has been considered, and what changes should prompt a fresh evaluation.

This matters when treatment is not working. Before adding another medication, clinicians may need to review whether the diagnosis remains accurate, whether the treatment trial was adequate, and whether sleep, medical illness, adverse effects, access barriers, or environmental stressors are influencing the outcome.

## Training, scope, and teamwork

Specialty training provides depth, but it does not eliminate blind spots. Psychiatric clinicians need to recognize medical causes of psychiatric symptoms, and clinicians in other specialties need to recognize the role of mental health in physical illness. Continuing education, interdisciplinary case review, and appropriate consultation can expand the perspective available to each patient.

Scope of practice and licensure requirements shape how services are delivered, especially across state lines and in telehealth. Effective collaboration requires respect for those boundaries, clear referral pathways, and an understanding that no single clinician is expected to provide every service a patient might need.

## Workload, professional strain, and outcome measures

Whole person assessment requires attention. Excessive administrative work and demanding workloads may make reflective reasoning and care coordination more difficult. Organizations should consider whether staffing, documentation support, and scheduling make careful clinical decisions feasible.

Documentation should show why a diagnosis was considered, what important alternatives were evaluated, and why a particular course of action was chosen. Appropriate acknowledgement of uncertainty and a clear follow up plan are signs of sound clinical practice, not weaknesses.

Standardized symptom measures can help monitor treatment, but they do not tell the whole story. Improvement should also be considered in terms of functioning, safety, relationships, quality of life, and the goals a patient identifies as important.

## The patient’s circumstances belong in the assessment

A treatment plan may be clinically reasonable and still be difficult to follow. Cost, transportation, unstable housing, caregiving obligations, language barriers, adverse effects, health literacy, and cultural expectations can all affect participation in care.

When a patient has not followed a plan, it is worth asking what stood in the way before concluding that the patient was unwilling. Was the medication affordable? Were the instructions understood? Did side effects interfere with work or family responsibilities? Could the patient access follow up care? These questions make the plan more realistic and often reveal needs that another prescription cannot resolve.

Patient participation in diagnostic decisions is not optional decoration. It helps clinicians understand what symptoms mean in daily life and whether proposed treatment goals are achievable. The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2015) specifically emphasized the role of patients and families as participants in the diagnostic process.

## Collaborative care can make the wider picture actionable

Comprehensive care does not mean one provider must manage everything. It means recognizing which concerns require another professional’s expertise and ensuring that the next clinician receives a coherent account of the problem and the plan.

In a randomized trial involving 214 adults with depression and poorly controlled diabetes, coronary heart disease, or both, Katon et al. (2010) evaluated a coordinated care intervention led by nurses working with primary care clinicians. Over 12 months, the intervention group had better combined results for depression symptoms and selected cardiometabolic measures than the usual care group. The trial supports the value of that particular integrated approach; it should not be taken as proof that every model of collaborative care will produce identical outcomes.

The practical lessons are familiar: clear treatment goals, timely referral feedback, consistent medication review, and shared responsibility for follow up. For complex cases, a brief discussion between professionals may resolve uncertainty that several isolated encounters could not.

## A holistic approach still requires evidence

Looking beyond the diagnostic label should not encourage indiscriminate testing, unproven therapies, or delays in effective treatment. A comprehensive approach remains selective. Decisions should reflect the patient’s clinical presentation, the quality of available evidence, potential harms, and informed preferences.

Sleep, nutrition, activity, psychological treatment, and social support can be important dimensions of care, but the evidence for specific interventions varies by condition. We owe patients an honest distinction between interventions supported by strong evidence and those that remain uncertain or experimental.

## Conclusion

A diagnosis is essential to clinical practice, but it should not be the endpoint of clinical curiosity. The patient with depression may also have a sleep disorder, chronic pain, or sustained social stress. A patient whose symptoms persist may need a fresh diagnostic review as well as a treatment adjustment. Someone who struggles with a care plan may need practical support before another intervention is added.

Our responsibility extends beyond assigning an appropriate diagnostic label. It includes examining alternative explanations, recognizing the limits of our own perspective, working across disciplines, and measuring improvement in ways that matter to the individual.

A diagnosis helps us decide where to begin. The patient tells us why we must keep looking.

## Five questions worth asking before closing the assessment

For complex presentations, or when the clinical course does not match expectations, I propose the following questions as a reminder to reconsider the wider picture. They are not a validated diagnostic scale. They reflect established principles of careful clinical reasoning and patient involvement.

1. What else could explain these symptoms? Consider reasonable alternatives and coexisting conditions.

2. What is contributing to the patient’s experience? Review sleep, medical history, medications, substances, functioning, and life circumstances.

3. What must not be missed? Identify red flags, urgent risks, and indications for focused testing or referral.

4. What can be treated now, and what needs to be reassessed? Avoid unnecessary delays while making diagnostic uncertainty explicit.

5. How will the patient recognize meaningful improvement? Consider daily functioning and personal goals alongside symptom scores.

The point is not to make every assessment longer. It is to make each clinical decision more attentive to what we know, what remains uncertain, and what the patient needs next.
